Philosophy

Programming is becoming cheaper.

Engineering judgment is becoming more valuable. That is the whole argument. Everything on this page follows from it.

Three ideas

Idea 01

Generated is not the same as understood.

Code that runs is evidence that something was produced. It is not evidence that anyone knows why it works, when it stops working, or what it quietly assumes.

Idea 02

The job is not to produce the most code. It is to produce the right system.

When implementation is cheap, the expensive mistakes move upstream — to what was built, why, and for whom.

Idea 03

Technology changes. Engineering reasoning transfers.

A framework has a shelf life. The ability to model a problem, weigh options and defend a decision does not. We teach the second one.

Engineering judgment

Every system can be wrong in two directions.

Judging a system means knowing how much engineering the actual requirements deserve — no more, no less.

Too little

Underengineered

Less engineering than the requirements demand.

  • Weak validation
  • Poor security
  • No failure handling
  • Fragile architecture
  • Missing observability

Just enough

Proportional

The engineering matches the requirements — and you know when they'll change.

  • Simple where it can be
  • Rigorous where it must be
  • Decisions written down
  • Known triggers for change

Too much

Overengineered

More engineering than the requirements justify.

  • Unnecessary microservices
  • Premature scaling
  • Excessive abstraction
  • Unnecessary dependencies
  • Solving problems that don't exist

Build the simplest system that adequately satisfies the actual requirements — and know when that simplicity must change.

Same system, different requirements

The bigger diagram is not the better one.

Two designs for the same product. Neither is correct in the abstract. Each is correct — or wrong — for its requirements.

Designed for 100 users

Application

What this buys: a system one person can hold in their head, deploy in minutes and debug at 2 a.m.

What would change it: sustained load, a second team, or an availability requirement this design can't meet.

What this buys: headroom, failure isolation and the ability to see what's happening under load.

What it costs: every extra box is something to run, secure, monitor and understand. Built for 100 users, it's a mistake.

The questions we keep asking

If you can't answer these, the code isn't finished.

  • Can you explain the system you just built?
  • What happens when it fails?
  • Why this architecture?
  • What are you assuming?
  • What happens at 10× scale?
  • Is this actually necessary?
  • What did AI decide for you?
  • Do you agree with it?
  • Can you defend the decision?

Disagree? Good.

These are positions, not slogans. If you think one is wrong, bring us the case.
